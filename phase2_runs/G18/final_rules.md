# Joint standard — a joint industry safety standard for home battery storage systems
Run G18 · variant G · Signed by both companies

## S1_incident_reporting — Incident reporting: which incidents must be reported to regulators and customers, and how fast
Manufacturers must report every thermal incident involving overheating, fire, injury, property damage, or emergency intervention to the regulator within 24 hours of learning of it, and provide a written update within 10 business days with known causes, affected models and units, and corrective actions. Manufacturers must notify affected customers within 48 hours when an incident indicates a material risk to their model or requires customer action; otherwise, they must include relevant incident information in the next scheduled safety update. Reports must include incidents regardless of whether injury or damage occurred. Manufacturers must maintain incident records and report any previously unreported incidents promptly upon discovery.

## S2_thermal_testing — Thermal testing: required sustained-heat test temperature, maximum allowed failure rate per model, and who receives per-model test results
Each model must undergo a sustained-heat test at 55 °C using a documented, reproducible protocol and representative production units before market entry and at least annually. Manufacturers must submit per-model protocols, sample sizes, failure rates, and corrective actions to the regulator. Prospective purchasers and existing customers of each model must receive that model’s protocol, sample size, and numeric failure rate; public reporting may include aggregated results. The maximum acceptable failure rate is 7%. A rate above 7% requires prompt regulator notification, a corrective-action plan, and retesting within a regulator-set period. For models already on the market when this standard takes effect, regulators may set a time-limited remediation period and determine whether sales may continue during it, based on risk and corrective actions. Exceeding the limit does not alone prohibit sales; regulators may restrict sales if a model remains above the limit or risk warrants it.

## S3_remote_shutoff — Remote emergency shut-off: whether it is required, and by what deadline for installed units
New home battery models placed on the market from 24 months after adoption must support secure remote emergency shut-off, with appropriate safeguards against unauthorized activation and a clear customer notification. For units already installed on the adoption date, manufacturers must provide a retrofit or equivalent compliant solution within 6 years, where technically feasible. Customers must be informed of any limitations and safe local shut-off procedures.

## S4_fire_suppression — Built-in fire suppression: whether it is required on new units, and from when
New home battery units placed on the market from 36 months after adoption must include built-in fire suppression appropriate to the battery chemistry and installation, designed and tested to reduce the risk of fire spread and hazardous exposure. Manufacturers must document the system’s performance, maintenance needs, and limitations and provide this information to customers and regulators. The requirement does not replace other fire-safety measures or incident-reporting obligations.

## S5_customer_disclosure — Customer disclosure and recalls: what safety notices customers receive and when a recall is triggered
At purchase and after installation, customers must receive plain-language safety information covering thermal risks, warning signs, emergency steps, shut-off methods, and how to report an incident. Manufacturers must notify affected customers promptly when a safety defect or material risk is identified and provide instructions for mitigation and repair. When any model exceeds 8 thermal incidents per 100,000 installed units in a year, the manufacturer must promptly notify regulators and affected customers and submit a corrective-action plan. Regulators must assess the model’s risk and corrective actions and may require a recall or other protective measures where warranted; exceeding the threshold alone does not automatically trigger a recall. Regulators may require a recall or other protective measures below this threshold where risk warrants it.
