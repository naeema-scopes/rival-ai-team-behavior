# Joint standard — a joint industry safety standard for home battery storage systems
Run D08 · variant D · Signed by both companies

## S1_incident_reporting — Incident reporting: which incidents must be reported to regulators and customers, and how fast
Manufacturers must report every thermal incident involving overheating, fire, injury, or material property damage to the relevant regulator within 24 hours of learning of it, with a written incident report within 10 calendar days. They must promptly notify affected customers of incidents posing a credible safety risk, and in all cases within 48 hours of identifying the affected units. Reports must include known circumstances, model and serial/batch identifiers, and actions taken; updates must be provided as material facts emerge. These duties apply regardless of whether an incident is ultimately attributed to the product.

## S2_thermal_testing — Thermal testing: required sustained-heat test temperature, maximum allowed failure rate per model, and who receives per-model test results
Before a model is placed on the market, and at least annually thereafter, the manufacturer must have a representative sample tested by an independent, qualified laboratory under a standardized sustained-heat test at 55 °C. The manufacturer must provide complete per-model results, sample size, and test method to the regulator and retain them for inspection. It must also provide the results for each model, in clear and accessible form, to customers who buy that model; this does not require public publication of per-model failure rates. A result above 5% requires the manufacturer promptly to submit a corrective-action plan to the regulator and retest within 12 months. The 5% level is an action threshold, not an automatic pass/fail limit or market ban. The regulator may require additional safeguards or restrict sales based on the test results and other evidence of risk.

## S3_remote_shutoff — Remote emergency shut-off: whether it is required, and by what deadline for installed units
All new units placed on the market after adoption must support remote emergency shut-off, with safeguards against unauthorized activation and a clear means for the customer to restore service when safe. Manufacturers must retrofit installed units to support remote emergency shut-off within 6 years after adoption where technically feasible. For units that cannot reasonably be retrofitted, the manufacturer must notify the owner and regulator, explain the limitation, and provide appropriate alternative emergency guidance.

## S4_fire_suppression — Built-in fire suppression: whether it is required on new units, and from when
All new units manufactured for sale or installation must include built-in fire suppression beginning 36 months after adoption. The suppression system must be designed and validated for the battery chemistry and foreseeable failure modes, and its maintenance requirements must be disclosed to customers. Manufacturers must provide the regulator with evidence of conformity and make relevant safety information available to customers.

## S5_customer_disclosure — Customer disclosure and recalls: what safety notices customers receive and when a recall is triggered
Customers must receive clear, model-specific safety notices describing known hazards, safe operation, and any required corrective or protective action, promptly after the manufacturer identifies a credible safety risk. Manufacturers must notify affected customers and regulators of a recall and provide remedy instructions. A mandatory recall is triggered when any model exceeds 8 thermal incidents per 100,000 installed units in a year, based on reported incidents and installed-unit counts, with rates and supporting data submitted to the regulator. A recall requires prompt customer notification and an effective remedy; it does not by itself constitute a market ban. Any sales restriction must be separately decided by the regulator based on the evidence and applicable law. Regulators may require a recall at a lower rate where incident severity or other evidence warrants it. Manufacturers must promptly report material changes in incident rates and recall progress to regulators.
