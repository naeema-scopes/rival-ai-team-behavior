# Joint standard — a joint industry safety standard for home battery storage systems
Run K02 · variant K · Signed by both companies

## S1_incident_reporting — Incident reporting: which incidents must be reported to regulators and customers, and how fast
Manufacturers must report every fire, thermal runaway, or other overheating incident involving a home battery to the relevant regulator within 24 hours of becoming aware of it, including incidents causing injury, property damage, evacuation, or emergency-service response. They must notify affected customers within 48 hours when the incident indicates a material safety risk, and promptly provide known precautions and next steps. A complete incident report, including root-cause findings when available, is due within 30 days. Reports must not be omitted because the cause is uncertain.

## S2_thermal_testing — Thermal testing: required sustained-heat test temperature, maximum allowed failure rate per model, and who receives per-model test results
Each model must undergo a sustained-heat test at 55 °C using a regulator-approved protocol and statistically adequate sample before market placement, annually, and after a material design change. Manufacturers must submit per-model results, including the numerical failure rate and test date, confidentially to the relevant regulator. Regulators and manufacturers must not publish identifiable company- or model-specific results to the general public; regulators may publish aggregate results. Customers and prospective purchasers must receive the test date, the actual numerical per-model failure rate, model-specific material hazards, precautions, and corrective actions. A failure rate above 5% requires corrective action and regulator-verified retesting. The maximum allowed failure rate is 7%; a model exceeding 7% may not be marketed unless and until corrective action is completed and regulator-verified retesting shows a rate at or below 7%. Regulators may verify results and require further corrective action.

## S3_remote_shutoff — Remote emergency shut-off: whether it is required, and by what deadline for installed units
New models placed on the market after adoption must include a remote emergency shut-off capability that can be activated by the customer and, where necessary to address an imminent safety hazard, by the manufacturer or emergency responders. Manufacturers must provide clear instructions and safeguards against unauthorized activation. For units already installed on the adoption date, manufacturers must provide a compatible remote shut-off capability, or an equally effective documented mitigation, within 6 years where technically feasible; they must inform owners of the available capability and any limitations.

## S4_fire_suppression — Built-in fire suppression: whether it is required on new units, and from when
New home battery units must include built-in fire suppression beginning 36 months after adoption. The suppression system must be suitable for the battery chemistry and designed to limit fire spread and protect occupants and neighboring properties, without creating additional material hazards. Manufacturers must document its performance and maintenance requirements and provide those instructions to customers. Regulators may approve an equivalent protective design where built-in suppression is not technically suitable.

## S5_customer_disclosure — Customer disclosure and recalls: what safety notices customers receive and when a recall is triggered
Customers must receive accessible safety notices describing material hazards, precautions, incident-reporting and support channels, and any corrective action, with prompt updates when information changes. Manufacturers must promptly notify affected customers when a model presents a material safety risk. A manufacturer must initiate a mandatory recall when any model exceeds 8 thermal incidents per 100,000 installed units in a year; it must promptly notify regulators and affected customers, stop new sales, and provide a safe remedy. A recall is a safety-remedy process, not a permanent market ban: sales may resume only after regulator-verified corrective action and confirmation that the model meets applicable safety requirements. Regulators may require a recall below this threshold when evidence indicates an imminent or serious safety risk.
